# Catalyst Facilitator Coaching and Interpretation Guide

## Purpose

This guide helps Catalyst facilitators, Beyond Code Collective coaches and instructors, and peer leaders interpret the Learner Pathway Alignment Tool in a consistent, strengths-first, non-gatekeeping way. The tool is designed to support reflection, coaching, confidence-building, pathway conversation, and program alignment. It is not a diagnosis, a validated psychological assessment, a hiring screen, a learning-style matching tool, or an automatic placement engine.

The facilitator’s role is to help the learner make meaning from the profile. The result should give the learner useful language for their strengths, support needs, and possible pathways. It should never be used to reduce the learner to a fixed type or to sort them away from opportunity.

## Core guardrails

- **Reflection first:** Treat every result as a starting point for conversation, not a final answer.
- **Strengths first:** Lead with what the learner brings, not what they lack.
- **No gatekeeping:** Use results to plan support, not to exclude learners from programs or pathways.
- **No prediction claims:** Do not say the tool predicts success, job performance, training completion, or career outcomes.
- **No fixed labels:** Do not describe archetypes, work styles, or motivation patterns as permanent traits.
- **No learning-style matching claims:** Do not say learners will learn better if instruction is matched to a preferred style.
- **Human review for placement:** Learners may receive profile results immediately, but placement and resource decisions should be confirmed through a facilitator conversation.
- **Context matters:** Interpret results alongside the learner’s lived circumstances, responsibilities, access to resources, and current support system.

## How the tool is structured

The current framework has three scored assessment modules, one universal design practice layer, and one program and partner alignment layer.

| Component | Purpose | Facilitator use |
|---|---|---|
| Module 1: Archetype identity | Identifies the learner’s strengths pattern across nine archetypes. | Use to name how the learner tends to contribute across contexts. |
| Module 2: Work style scenarios | Surfaces likely behavior in realistic work or learning situations. | Use to plan fit, support, communication, pacing, and team role conversations. |
| Module 3: Motivation and pathway orientation | Identifies what keeps the learner invested and derives pathway orientation. | Use to discuss placement, ownership, stability, risk comfort, and sustainability. |
| Universal design practice layer | Replaces the retired learning preferences assessment module. | Use to support self-advocacy, varied resources, and peer exchange for all learners. |
| Program and partner alignment layer | Connects learner profile patterns to available and future training tracks. | Use as an alignment aid, not as a scored assessment module. |

Learners complete all three scored modules in one intake and receive one combined reflection profile. They should not see module-by-module results during intake, and they should not receive technical score labels. Facilitators receive the combined profile plus module-level drill-downs for coaching and placement conversations.

## Results release and facilitator workflow

Learners should see their combined reflection profile immediately after completing all three scored modules. Facilitator review should gate placement decisions, not the learner’s access to their own self-knowledge.

Recommended workflow:

1. Learner completes all three scored modules.
2. Learner immediately receives the combined reflection profile.
3. Facilitator receives the facilitator-facing report with score detail, confidence flags, and module drill-downs.
4. Beyond Code Collective coaches and instructors follow up within 5 to 7 business days.
5. If Catalyst or another high-stakes placement is being considered, the system may generate a recommendation, but it should not auto-assign.
6. Placement and resource decisions are confirmed after a coaching conversation.

If the learner completes the intake outside facilitator working hours, the profile should still release immediately. Coaching outreach can happen the next business day.

## Coaching conversation structure

Use the profile to open a conversation, not to deliver a verdict.

### Opening

Start with learner ownership:

- “You saw your profile first. What parts felt true?”
- “What surprised you?”
- “Where did you think, yes, that sounds like me?”
- “Where did you think, I am not sure about that?”

### Strengths

Help the learner name what they already bring:

- “Where have you seen this strength show up before?”
- “When do people tend to come to you for help?”
- “What kind of work gives you energy once you get started?”
- “What do you do well that you sometimes overlook?”

### Support needs

Frame supports as normal planning, not remediation:

- “What conditions help this strength show up?”
- “What gets in the way of using this strength?”
- “Where might Beyond Code Collective coaches and instructors need to support you early?”
- “What should instructors or peer leaders know so you do not have to carry everything alone?”

### Pathway connection

Connect profile patterns to programs and partner tracks without overclaiming:

- “Based on this profile, these pathways may be worth exploring.”
- “This does not decide your placement by itself. It gives us better questions to ask.”
- “Which option feels energizing, and which one feels stable enough to actually sustain?”
- “What would make this path realistic for your life right now?”

### Close

End with one concrete next step:

- A program or partner track to explore.
- A skill area to try.
- A support plan to put in place.
- A conversation with an instructor, coach, peer leader, or program partner.
- A follow-up review after the learner has completed a real project or milestone.

## Module 1: Archetype identity interpretation

Module 1 identifies the learner’s primary identity-oriented strengths pattern across nine archetypes. It answers the question: who is this learner across contexts?

Do not treat the archetype as the whole learner. The archetype gives language for a strength pattern, not a box. A learner may see themselves in more than one archetype, and strengths may shift as the learner gains experience.

### Archetype quick reference

| Archetype | Core strength | Watch for | Coaching question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Navigator | Direction, purpose, bigger picture, next steps. | May wait for clarity before acting. | “What is the next useful step, even if the full path is not clear yet?” |
| Developer | Building, fixing, testing, making ideas real. | May build before clarifying purpose or user need. | “Who is this for, and how will you know it works?” |
| Igniter | Starting, momentum, action, first steps. | May need structure for follow-through. | “What support turns this strong start into a finished result?” |
| Connector | Linking people, ideas, resources, and perspectives. | May overextend or become the unpaid glue. | “How can you connect others without losing focus on your own goals?” |
| Systems Thinker | Patterns, causes, structure, root problems. | May overanalyze before deciding. | “What is enough information to take the next step?” |
| Culture Keeper | Group mood, belonging, trust, steadiness. | May carry emotional labor for the group. | “What boundaries help you support the group without running yourself empty?” |
| Designer | Usability, clarity, experience, how something works for the user. | May polish too long before testing. | “What feedback would help you improve this with real users?” |
| Support Specialist | One-on-one help, troubleshooting, patient explanation. | May be typecast as helper instead of advancing their own goals. | “How can this support strength grow into technical skill or leadership?” |
| Explorer | Curiosity, experimentation, trying paths before committing. | May sample without going deep. | “What small experiment would give you evidence about what fits?” |

### Module 1 confidence and flag language

Facilitators may see operational flags such as blended profile, broad high pattern, flat pattern, low confidence primary, or tie among several archetypes. These are staff-facing interpretation aids only. Do not repeat those technical labels to learners.

Use developmental learner-safe language:

- For a flat or low-confidence result: “Your strengths are still taking shape. This is normal, especially when you are exploring new environments. Beyond Code Collective coaches and instructors will help you build on what is already showing up.”
- For a broad high result: “Your responses show strengths across many areas. This often means you adapt to different situations or bring range. Beyond Code Collective coaches and instructors will help you identify where to focus first.”
- For several possible strengths: “Your pattern is showing range across several strengths. This is a starting point for a coaching conversation, not a final picture.”

Facilitator interpretation:

- **Blended profile:** The learner has two or more meaningful strengths that should be discussed together.
- **Broad high pattern:** The learner may bring range, adaptability, or strong agreement tendencies. Use conversation to identify where the strengths are most alive in practice.
- **Flat pattern:** The learner may be exploring, uncertain, guarded, unfamiliar with the item language, or genuinely variable across contexts. Do not frame as lack of strengths.
- **Low-confidence primary:** Use coaching conversation and real project evidence before making strong interpretive claims.

## Module 2: Work style interpretation

Module 2 uses forced-choice scenarios to surface how a learner is likely to respond in realistic learning, tech, or workplace situations. It answers the question: how does this learner tend to work in context?

Each axis has two legitimate poles. Neither pole is better, more professional, more mature, or more tech-ready.

### Module 2 axes

| Axis | Pole 1 | Pole 2 | Facilitator interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Social energy | Solo | Collaborative | Where the learner tends to find clarity and energy while working. |
| Structure preference | Structured | Adaptive | Whether the learner prefers clear expectations or room to work out the path. |
| Contribution mode | Front-facing | Behind the scenes | Whether the learner gravitates toward visible representation or building/supporting the work out of view. |
| Pace | Quick-moving | Methodical | Whether the learner tends to iterate quickly or proceed carefully and thoroughly. |

### Interpreting 3-to-0 versus 2-to-1 results

Each Module 2 axis has three scenarios.

| Pattern | Meaning | Facilitator use |
|---|---|---|
| 3-to-0 | Clear lean or clean sweep toward one pole. | Treat as a stronger signal, but still confirm through conversation. |
| 2-to-1 | Lighter lean toward one pole. | Treat as a preference with flexibility, not a hard pattern. |

Because there are three scenarios per axis, there is no true mathematical balanced result in the current version. Unless Catalyst explicitly creates a “balanced with a lean” display rule, facilitators should interpret every axis as a lean with signal strength.

### Coaching by axis

| Axis and pole | Strength to name | Support to consider |
|---|---|---|
| Solo | Focus, independent problem-solving, deep work. | Make sure the learner is connected enough to receive input and not disappear when stuck. |
| Collaborative | Thinking aloud, shared energy, group problem-solving. | Build solo focus time so progress does not depend entirely on others being available. |
| Structured | Clarity, planning, reliability, efficient execution. | Practice adapting when plans change or expectations are incomplete. |
| Adaptive | Flexibility, improvisation, comfort with open-ended work. | Add enough structure to support follow-through and completion. |
| Front-facing | Communication, representation, visibility, point-of-contact energy. | Check that visible contribution is backed by substance, preparation, and support. |
| Behind the scenes | Building, craft, depth, operational contribution. | Make sure the learner receives credit, visibility, and advancement opportunities. |
| Quick-moving | Momentum, iteration, willingness to get a version done. | Practice slowing down when accuracy, safety, or quality requires it. |
| Methodical | Care, precision, thoroughness, quality control. | Practice first drafts, deadlines, and moving before everything is perfect. |

### Module 2 sustainability and anti-gatekeeping rule

Module 2 is especially useful for sustainable fit, not just initial fit. A learner may be capable of a track but need specific supports because the track’s pace, structure, social demands, or contribution mode creates strain.

Use mismatch as a support-planning signal:

- Methodical learner in a fast-moving track: plan pacing strategies, early check-ins, and clear minimum viable expectations.
- Quick-moving learner in a slow or heavily structured track: plan challenge, visible progress markers, and ways to reduce boredom or friction.
- Structured learner in an adaptive track: provide scaffolds, examples, and check-in points.
- Adaptive learner in a structured track: explain where structure is non-negotiable and where there is room for choice.

If two or more Module 2 dimensions oppose a track profile, flag the pattern for facilitator attention. Do not block the learner from the track on that basis. The right use is: “This may need support.” The wrong use is: “This learner does not belong here.”

## Module 3: Motivation and pathway orientation interpretation

Module 3 identifies what keeps the learner invested and derives a pathway orientation. It answers the question: what kind of path may serve this learner right now?

Module 3 has three sub-dimensions:

| Sub-dimension | What it means | Facilitator caution |
|---|---|---|
| Self-direction | How much the learner wants to build, own, and direct their own work. | High self-direction does not always mean the learner is ready to leap immediately. |
| Stability-seeking | How much the learner wants reliable, predictable footing. | Stability-seeking is not lesser and is not a verdict on capacity. |
| Risk comfort | How much uncertainty the learner can sit with. | Risk comfort is shaped by material circumstance and should never be read as pure personality. |

### Derived pathway patterns

Pathway orientation is derived from self-direction and stability-seeking. It is not measured as a separate score.

| Self-direction | Stability-seeking | Derived orientation | Coaching interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | Low | Ownership/building lean | Learner may be energized by shaping and directing their own work. Explore ownership paths, project leadership, or building toward running their own work. |
| Low | High | Placement/role lean | Learner may be energized by contributing inside a reliable role or organization. Explore stable tracks with growth and skill-building. |
| High | High | Blended or staged path | Learner may want to build something of their own and also need solid ground. Explore staged pathways that combine stability with ownership growth. |
| Low | Low | Still exploring | Learner may not have a strong pathway pull yet. Do not force a direction. Use coaching, exposure, and small experiments. |

### Risk comfort as a sustainability modifier

Risk comfort modifies sustainability and support planning. It should not redirect the learner away from an ownership or building path by itself.

Examples:

- High self-direction plus lower risk comfort: the learner may want to build but need scaffolding, a safety net, staged risk, income stability, or a side-build approach.
- High stability-seeking plus lower risk comfort: the learner may need predictable footing right now. This should be honored without closing future ownership paths.
- High self-direction plus high risk comfort: the learner may tolerate uncertainty well, but still needs planning, mentorship, and realistic expectations.
- High stability-seeking plus high risk comfort: the learner may want a stable base while still being capable of uncertainty when needed.

### M3-RSK-04 reverse-scored item

Facilitators do not need to discuss reverse scoring with learners unless the learner asks how scoring works. Fonz should clearly mark M3-RSK-04 as reverse-scored in the scoring map.

Item:

> M3-RSK-04: A long stretch of not knowing how things will turn out would wear on me.

Scoring map:

| Raw response | Raw value | Scored value |
|---|---:|---:|
| Strongly disagree | 1 | 5 |
| Disagree | 2 | 4 |
| Neutral / Not sure | 3 | 3 |
| Agree | 4 | 2 |
| Strongly agree | 5 | 1 |

Interpretation note: agreement with this item does not mean the learner lacks courage or ambition. It may mean sustained uncertainty is costly for them, especially if they have financial responsibility, family obligations, debt, unstable housing, or limited safety net.

### Module 3 equity guardrail

Risk comfort and stability-seeking are strongly shaped by material circumstance. A learner who wants stable footing may be making a wise and responsible decision for their life. A learner who reports lower risk comfort may still have strong ownership drive but may not be able to afford uncertainty right now.

Do not use learner-facing language that says or implies:

- “You are risk-averse.”
- “You are not suited to building your own thing.”
- “You should avoid ownership paths.”
- “You are not ready.”
- “You lack drive.”

Use language like:

- “This may be the version of the path that serves you right now.”
- “You may need a staged path with support and solid ground.”
- “Wanting stability can be wise, especially when you are building a foundation.”
- “Ownership can be something you build toward over time.”

## Universal design practice layer

The former Module 4 should not be used as a scored assessment module. The tool should not claim that teaching to a learner’s preferred channel improves learning outcomes. Learners may have real preferences, but the stronger learning-style matching claim should not be part of the assessment, report, or placement logic.

The useful goal is preserved as a universal design practice layer:

- Instructors, coaches, and peer leaders understand varied learning preferences without treating them as fixed learning styles.
- Students are helped to identify how they like to receive, practice, and revisit information.
- Resources are compiled in multiple formats.
- Peer exchange is structured so learners can access ideas through explanation, example, practice, repetition, and collaboration.
- Learners are supported in self-advocacy without being sorted by a learning-style label.

### Coaching prompts for learning preference self-advocacy

Use these as conversation prompts, not scored items:

- “When something is new or hard, what helps you understand it?”
- “What kind of explanation do you usually return to when you are stuck?”
- “Do you learn better by seeing an example, hearing someone explain it, reading steps, practicing it, or talking it through?”
- “What should instructors or peer leaders know about how you ask for help?”
- “What kind of resource would you actually use again after class?”
- “When you have to learn something technical, what helps you stay with it long enough for it to click?”

### Instructor, coach, and peer leader expectations

The implementation conversation should focus on practice:

- Build a shared resource bank across programs and partner tracks.
- Offer multiple ways into difficult content when possible.
- Encourage peer explanation and study exchange.
- Normalize learners asking for resources in different forms.
- Teach learners to notice what helps them learn without treating that preference as destiny.
- Review whether each track has enough varied supports for rigorous tech training.

## What facilitators should not do

Do not:

- Tell learners their archetype is their identity.
- Tell learners a work style makes them more or less suited for tech.
- Treat stability-seeking as lack of ambition.
- Treat lower risk comfort as lack of courage.
- Use pace mismatch to block a learner from a track.
- Use the tool as an automatic placement decision.
- Share technical labels such as low confidence primary, flat pattern, or facilitator review recommended with learners.
- Show learners raw scores, averages, percentages, or technical score tables unless Catalyst later makes a deliberate policy decision to do so.
- Claim the tool is validated beyond the evidence available from pilot and review.
- Claim that matching instruction to a learning style improves outcomes.
- Use “entrepreneur” as the default learner-facing word for ownership paths.

## Recommended facilitator language

Use:

- “This profile gives us language for what may already be showing up.”
- “This is not a box. It is a starting point.”
- “There are no better or worse poles here.”
- “This result helps us plan support, not decide your limits.”
- “A mismatch does not mean no. It means we plan for support.”
- “Wanting stability is wise for many people. It can be part of a long-term building path.”
- “Beyond Code Collective coaches and instructors can help you build on what is already showing up.”

Avoid:

- “This means you are...”
- “You are not a fit for...”
- “Your result needs review.”
- “You scored low.”
- “You are weak in...”
- “You should avoid...”
- “You are risk-averse.”
- “You are a learning style.”

## Quick-reference checklist

Before a coaching conversation:

- Review the combined profile.
- Review module-level drill-downs.
- Check for blended, broad high, flat, or low-confidence Module 1 flags.
- Check Module 2 3-to-0 versus 2-to-1 signal strength.
- Check whether any Module 2 dimensions may oppose the likely track profile.
- Check Module 3 pathway orientation and risk comfort sustainability notes.
- Look for any signs that material circumstance may be shaping stability or risk responses.
- Prepare 2 to 3 strengths-first questions.

During the conversation:

- Let the learner react first.
- Name strengths before supports.
- Ask for real examples.
- Treat the profile as a conversation tool.
- Connect patterns to possible pathways without overclaiming.
- Discuss support needs as normal planning.
- End with one concrete next step.

After the conversation:

- Record any learner corrections or context.
- Note whether the profile felt accurate, partially accurate, or unclear.
- Confirm placement or pathway recommendation only after the conversation.
- Add support notes for instructors, coaches, and peer leaders.
- Flag any item or output language that confused the learner for pilot review.

## Final facilitator principle

The tool should help learners leave with more language, more confidence, and more support than they had before they started. If an interpretation makes a learner feel boxed in, judged, delayed, or sorted out of opportunity, the interpretation is being used wrong. The right use is to name strengths, surface support needs, and help the learner take a next step that is both ambitious and realistic for their life right now.
